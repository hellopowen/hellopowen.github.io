Industrial design—
from form-giving to product definition.

My practice began in Taipei and Milan, then developed in Shanghai, where market, commercial organisation, design and R&D, and supply chain met within one product-development environment. I began with form and detail, then learned how design intent works at different scales: individual products, product families, ventures and global design-language systems. Today I bring that discipline into product definition, where physical controls and growing digital capability must become one coherent experience.

How the work develops

Give an idea enough form to be examined.

I begin by asking what a product should offer the person using it. Research can change that understanding; sketches and prototypes let people respond to a possible answer. As the work develops, I use what we learn to guide the details and help the team decide what matters. Across a product family, that becomes a set of shared principles other designers can work with. The purpose stays open to question as it takes form.

01 / Philips Experience Design / Amsterdam + Shanghai / 2019

One Philips family, across different product worlds.

Making coffee, caring for clothes and cleaning the air bring people into different relationships with their appliances. We developed shared design principles that could make Philips recognisable across those encounters while giving each category room to express its own purpose.

Philips brings coffee, cooking, garment care and air care together under one domestic-appliance brand.

Versuni · Philips brand context ↗

Philips Design has repeatedly made a changing view of life and technology tangible—from Vision of the Future in 1996 to Next Simplicity in 2005/06. The philosophy evolved rather than preserving one look. In the 2019 language work, the scale was the product family: begin with the whole form, then work inward through detail, material, interaction, light and graphics.

The common framework read every category through the same five layers. It gave designers and business teams a shared way to compare unlike products, review quality and decide what should travel. Perceptual and experiential principles could be carried across; the architecture that made each category credible remained specific.

The framework stayed constant while each answer changed. Air could move from professional to approachable through its raised base, visible air semantics, circular light and fabric. Coffee could make process visible through transparency, amber light and ingredient focus.

The work joined category direction, interaction, CMF and brand around a tangible guideline. My contribution covered Air in the Amsterdam formulation and part of the collection work developed in Shanghai; the final synthesis was shared work across five lead designers and three design directors.

The system was issued across Domestic Appliances. A separate 2021 engagement brought me back to apply it to categories Philips had not produced before. That was the useful test of governance: the five lenses could guide new work without prescribing an existing product or repeating one shape.

Role and reach

  • One of the global lead designers on the Philips Domestic Appliances design-language system in 2019.
  • The language covered air, kitchen appliances, garment care and floor care. Floor care is system scope, not a claim of Philips floor-care product design.
  • Returned through a separate 2021 engagement to apply the language to new categories.

02 / BMW Group Designworks, Shanghai / 2015—2016

Product character, from first impression to touch.

The character of a product changes as someone moves around it, picks it up or puts it on. At Designworks, I developed selected directions through curves, surfaces and component details, working to carry the intended character into the way a wearable, tricycle/stroller or tablet would be encountered and used.

For 37 Degree, a common sensing unit had to become a credible body-worn product rather than an electronic module attached to a generic strap. I carried substantial 2D and 3D development across the frame, body contact, interaction, locking detail, material and wearing architecture, working with manager and team support.

The work moved through metal-frame, soft-band and adhesive relationships into CNC metal samples, wearing prototypes and a later band-definition phase. The programme was delivered but did not launch; a PCB was prototyped, while activated electronics are not established.

The BMW-licensed two-in-one tricycle/stroller applied the same discipline at a larger scale. The selected concept was inherited; my work occupied the substantial distance between that direction and a detailed product architecture. A split central frame had to hold across child-controlled and parent-guided configurations, while the seat, rear structure, controls, storage and accessories remained one product.

Repeated and radiating lines were built through controlled curvature rather than simple offsets. The centre cover and pouch were developed line by line so layered surfaces, crease and tension could read coherently while the CAD moved towards engineering development.

Jide posed a different question: what should belong to the tablet and what could move outside it? Integrated stands made mechanism, ergonomics, structure, thickness and identity one architectural problem. As the proposition narrowed towards a thinner tablet and separate support accessory, design value had to move rather than disappear.

The late direction retained a restrained pair of curves around the palm areas and a deliberate relationship with the cover and stand. It matches the Remix Pro publicly unveiled in 2016, whose industrial design was credited to Designworks; that studio credit does not turn the programme into a sole-authorship claim.

The same studio discipline could make a future service tangible.

In the On-Demand Mobility study, my contribution began after the field research. We reorganised insights around passenger, driver and operator, then built three future journeys connecting service, digital identity and the physical vehicle. The journey work located each need before proposals responded; a later matrix helped prioritise them by usage frequency and relevance.

Role and boundaries

  • Designer at BMW Group Designworks, Shanghai, from 2015 to 2016.
  • 37 Degree was delivered through physical samples but did not launch; no activated electronics prototype is claimed.
  • The tricycle/stroller contribution developed an inherited direction through 2D, 3D, component integration and advanced CAD; initial authorship and launch are not claimed.
  • Jide connects architectural alternatives to the publicly unveiled Remix Pro at product level; individual authorship and retail shipment remain bounded.
  • The ODM contribution began with research insights, not fieldwork, and no influence on a later BMW service or vehicle is claimed.

03 / Philips Experience Design, Shanghai / role 2016—2020 · venture 2017—2020

A more personal way to wear protection.

A mask is worn against the face and seen by everyone around it; its appearance matters alongside the comfort of wearing it. We developed a soft textile shell around facial geometry, seeking protection that people could feel at ease wearing while resolving weight, contact and the working technology.

Philips Fresh Air Mask · product and lifestyle photographs at iF Design ↗

Breathing comfort depends on exhaling, so warm, damp air needed a route out. Consumer work added another requirement: the technology had to be visibly useful. The fan did not need to disappear; its presence made the function legible and became part of how the mask was socially read.

The fan and battery were split into separate chambers to balance weight and release space for exhalation. Five facial anchor points identified where the product could take load without making the face uncomfortable. Informed by facial-scan data, those points became a parametric geometry that could work with variation rather than an average face.

Fit, airflow, filtration, acoustics, textile, electronics, charging and social meaning had to resolve together. The design role was to keep those specialist decisions accountable to one experience on the body.

Softness had to be engineered.

The soft shell took four attempts. Cut-and-sewn construction needed too many seams; a bonded preform held the wrong rigidity and odour. The thermoplastic-yarn route let us tune softness and structural support through the yarn composition, mould temperature and the behaviour of male and female tooling. The shell had to remain comfortable against the face while carrying the internal components.

Material was both utility and expression. The same development joined knitting, edge construction, breathability, supplier samples and manufacturing with the way the product looked and felt when worn.

The satisfaction was seeing design intent, engineering and execution work together in the finished product.

I was the product designer in the eight-person founding venture team, working from the 2017 pitch through the January 2020 launch. The scope crossed idea pitch, commercialisation, product development, supply-chain integration and realisation, including direction of the packaging design through to manufacture.

On the record

  • Design registrations name me as creator on the mask across WIPO, Taiwan and Malaysia.
  • China utility model CN211461836U covers the mask shell and names me among the inventors.
  • The programme received recognition credited to Philips Experience Design; Dutch Design Week 2020 named the project and its designers.

04 / Studio Kohler, Shanghai / role 2010—2014

Wellness through the experience of water.

How water reaches the body can shape the experience of a bathroom. Through Beitou's water and material development and a separate massage-informed shower study, I explored how fixtures could support wellbeing through the movement, delivery and presence of water.

Beitou faucet · product photographs and award record at iF Design ↗

Beitou developed slow water movement and cast glass together. The water was not an effect added after the form: the length and section of the spout, the material, the control and the way water became visible were one proposition within a residential and hospitality setting.

At the premium end, quality had to be legible in more than finish. The development moved between sketches, product types, water studies, physical models, CMF alternatives and complete bathroom views. That sequence let the team judge proportion, material, water behaviour and architectural context as one experience.

A separate collaboration with Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine translated massage references into body locations, spray configurations, full-size prototypes and engineering tests. The work moved from research to bodily sensation and then into engineering: water became utility, material expression and interaction rather than decoration applied to a plumbing fixture.

Hospitality context did not turn the product into architecture. It made the product accountable to architecture—to the interior, its proportions and finishes, and to the sequence by which a person approaches, touches and uses water.

My contribution belongs to the research and development undertaken during my role. The collection launched after I left Kohler; an official iF record dates its market launch to 2015, while the later 2016 publication names the final product and its contributors. Those records remain separate from my development contribution.

Role and reach

  • Senior Designer at Studio Kohler, Shanghai, from 2010 to 2014.
  • Work included research-led showering, premium water-delivery development and first-generation filtration.
  • A signed Kohler disclosure names me among four inventors on a premium faucet and shower range; final-product authorship remains product-specific.

05 / Philips Air + Homecare / Shanghai + Amsterdam / 2018—2019

Bringing air care into everyday living.

In 2018, Philips Air's promise was moving from “Healthy air, always” to “Better air, better quality of life”: effective purification also needed a considered place in the home. I developed the range language around how people would see, approach and use it, giving two sub-ranges distinct characters on a shared scalable architecture.

Philips Urban Living range · product photographs at iF Design ↗

A common architecture made the range possible and made sameness more likely. The products still had to separate into two channel-specific sub-ranges, across three sizes, without losing the family.

At a distance, a purifier communicates through silhouette, proportion and the light ring that makes air quality visible. Closer, the control comes into reach. At touch distance, material, perforation and grille detail take over. We designed the range across those distances rather than treating form, interface and CMF as separate layers.

Fabric was positioned on the upper body as a human touchpoint, where the product is seen and a hand naturally reaches. The functional intake remained below. A perforation field between 2.8 and 3.8 millimetres dissolved into the fabric above and the base below, while the top surface brought outlet, control and feedback together.

The decision gave products that could converge at a distance a different domestic presence. It also gave two sub-ranges room to move: one wrapping the material horizontally, the other carrying it vertically through a more tapered body.

Series 800 · product and lifestyle photographs at iF Design ↗

  • The Urban Living range was adopted and shipped; the Series 800 interpretation followed through another designer.

06 / KISKA, Shanghai / 2023

Designing for the life a family is moving towards.

In conversations in Jiangyou, Yiwu and Baoding, people's aspirations often centred on providing for family and the home they hoped to build. We revised the research imagery to explore those ambitions, then developed refrigerator CMF directions from the life stages participants described.

The interview material brought people, interiors, activities, colour, material and aspiration into one montage. Participants could take it apart: what belonged to their lives now, what did not, and what they were moving towards.

After the first conversations, we reduced the saturation and rebuilt the imagery around what people had taught us: work and family setting, where they had come from, whether they hoped to buy a home, and what came next. Returning to Shanghai between cities let the next conversation go further instead of repeating a board that had already reached its limit.

The six directions became six aesthetic statements grounded in customer language. Expert material and process input then helped translate them into colour, finish, pattern, texture and transferable production possibilities.

The final proposals applied all six directions to refrigerators and compared their role across a portfolio. They were delivered as CMF and graphic directions; no adoption or market result is claimed.

07 / Vehicle product definition / United Kingdom / 2024—

Making digital capability meaningful in the cabin.

Growing digital capability creates more possibilities for the cabin, but each function still needs a clear place in someone's experience. My work connects the intended experience with physical controls and display behaviour, using shared access, enjoyment and confidence to help judge what belongs and what should be left out. I translate those priorities into interaction logic and product requirements that creative, interface, human-factors and engineering teams can develop together.

Two pre-release examples require opposite kinds of judgement. One connects a central physical control with two displays as a shared driver-and-passenger interaction. The other asks how a performance HUD can do more with less in a constrained supercar viewport—using minimal graphical cues rather than becoming a second infotainment screen or duplicating the instrument cluster.

The programmes remain pre-release. This portfolio shows only public brand context and the permitted design questions: no programme or display names, current vehicle imagery, internal artefacts or unannounced outcomes.

About

Design enters people's daily lives.

I grew up in Hualien, Taiwan, and spent part of my childhood in my maternal grandparents' bicycle shop. Watching them make a bicycle work well showed me how much care practical craft requires.

Pottery was my first way of communicating through something tangible. Industrial design then taught me to work through form, proportion and scale. Working in Shanghai brought the practice close to research, engineering, supply chains and manufacture.

At NYU, the practice extended into code and connected media. Today I bring that range into product definition and design governance: making intent usable across disciplines while staying close enough to form, control and detail to judge whether the final experience still holds.

Based in Berkshire, United Kingdom. Product Experience Lead at McLaren Automotive.
