0012025–

McLaren Automotive: contesting the feature list

Experience designer · Product Experience Lead

Product definition written from what a driver gets, on a programme where the supplier arrives with the answer already shaped.

The problem

Product definition defaults to engineering, and for a good reason: feasibility lives there. The supplier brings a solution that already works, the solution fits the schedule, and the feature is settled before anyone has asked how it connects to the rest of the experience. We are outnumbered roughly ten to one. So the first design decision is about which two or three features are worth contesting, and about going further into those than anyone expects a designer to go.

A decision

We positioned the performance HUD for a supercar against two existing answers and took neither. Gulfstream immerses a pilot in two layers of visual guidance. The current automotive answer lets the display all but replace the instrument cluster. A supercar keeps its cluster, and its viewing port is compressed by a low roof.

The answer was to do less, to provide more.

The right signal at the right time, so the driver is not looking at the display and still gets everything from it. We mapped candidate features onto a quadrant of confidence, guidance, control and performance, against the two things a driver takes from the car: acuity, which guidance gives, and achievement, which only control and performance give. That set the feature list, and it is a different list from the benchmark’s. The instrument also has to survive procurement, lead time, manufacturing constraint and cost, which is what stops a feature list being a wish list.

What changed

The argument is now made in an instrument rather than in a meeting. A feature defined by what the customer takes from it survives a change of vehicle, of supplier and of organisation.

Forseven: the centre console nobody would define

Experience designer · Product Experience Lead

Cockpit and body-control definition on an electric-vehicle programme, taken on because nobody else would take it.

A start-up, in a product organisation alongside product strategy and product management. My side of the team was experience definition, my counterpart’s was product definition, and the centre console switch pack fell exactly between them. Design owned the look and the touch-feel. Systems engineering waited for design to say what the controls should do. Design held that the interaction sat outside its brief, which was true, and the gap stayed open.

The software platform arrived as functional definition sheets, one per interface, each describing what that interface could technically be made to do. I read them myself, because the alternative is letting capability decide the product. What came back was a definition: which functions belong on the console, how each behaves, and how the console connects driver and passenger as a shared cockpit instead of a driver’s cockpit with a passenger sitting in it. Every interaction was mapped onto the three layers of our experience strategy, and the interaction map was validated with human factors.

The component did not carry into McLaren. The method did.

0042023

Colour, material and finish direction for Haier refrigerators

Senior design manager, product experience design

Designing the surface of a major appliance for buyers in tier-three and tier-four China, where a global agency’s own aesthetic instrument does not read.

Haier wanted a CMF direction for refrigerator door panels aimed at tier-three and tier-four cities, designed from those buyers rather than adapted down to them. Three field stops, in Zhejiang, Sichuan and Hebei, found that what people could imagine a refrigerator looking like stopped at what was already on the shelf in front of them.

The harder constraint was ours. The palettes, the references and the aesthetic reasoning a global agency carries are learned in mature markets, and they do not transfer.

The innovation is going deeper into the local aesthetic, instead of assuming what people want by moving a global aesthetic into local dress.

So we built from local aspiration upward:

  • twenty-one interviews across three locations
  • three market visits
  • nine days in the field with a core team of three

We read lifestyle and life stage through image response, then translated aspiration into the tones, gradients and finishes those buyers actually read as premium, with CMF experts supplying the material processes that could carry them. The interviews and the expert view put those cities within three to five years of tier-one taste, so we iterated the boards live across the three stops, testing at each one how much newness people would take, and designed to where the taste was going rather than to where it was.

The output was six aesthetic statements, each with a colour and material story and a process that transfers to a production line. Haier reviewed them in October and took the final design drafts into their own team to execute. It was my last project in China.

0072016–2020

Intelligent Mask to Fresh Air Mask

Founding product designer

Philips Air was a clinical category, built on a medical idea of air. We went looking for the value space around it, through digital products, wearables and business-to-business ideas, and we came back with a mask. A mask reached the most people, it was personal to own, and it belonged to a company whose business is experience.

Deciding that was the easy half. Pitching it, and keeping it alive inside a large organisation until it shipped, was the venture.

A venture inside a corporation is judged twice, once as an idea and once as a business. So the work was turning research into a product that holds up aesthetically, functionally and emotionally, and that a supply chain could actually build at quality. As one of the eight in the founding venture team I sat on the venture committee that assessed supplier design and research capability, which is the design lead sitting in procurement, because how a thing is made is a design question before it is a commercial one.

Led the design from the 2017 incubation and the product-system architecture, through design research, to the first generation reaching the market. From the first search for a value space in 2016 to the January 2020 launch is three and a half years of keeping one idea alive inside a company that had not asked for it. The team was progressive rather than fixed: eight people across the journey, joining and leaving as the work asked for different skills, and each stage carried its own venture team behind it.

Named designer on the Philips Fresh Air Mask project at Dutch Design Week 2020. Named as Senior Product Designer in the Philips Fresh Air Mask documentary featured at Dutch Design Week 2020. Philips Experience Design’s own film of the project opens with me presenting it.

When the pandemic came, people reached for it for something I had not designed it for, and it held.

The family grew after I left. Product family · 2020–2022

Design strategy, the system, and the range

A product design language system is a strong, dynamic guideline that helps the brand experience be perceived, be felt, and be a benefit at every touchpoint a customer meets. It is dynamic because three things keep moving: the brand, the customer and the context.

0092018

The design language system for Philips Air

Product designer, Air category

Led the Product Design Language System for Philips Air quality and Homecare categories in 2018.

The brand was moving, and the system had to carry the move. Philips Air looked clinical and professional, and the destination was better air and better quality of life. Better quality of life is domestic. It is a choice a person makes about their own home, so the products had to be approachable as well as strong performers. I read that shift against the group’s design principles and turned it into a landscape the business could design into.

Three attributes carried the work, and choosing three rather than everything is the decision. Product interaction, including UI, UX and lighting design. CMF design. Product design itself. I worked with business, with engineering, with research and with the larger design team to investigate the next generation of product, and I ran the workshops with product management, planning and the cross-functional teams. Three groups produced three themes, each strong in a different area, and we synthesised them into one coherent set of principles, visualised and carried into a pilot. Form, proportion and form-giving carry all of it.

The system covered the air products, across hydro, core and combi. Urban Living is the core range. The mask stayed outside it deliberately, because a wearable does not obey the rules of a domestic appliance.

What it became

Philips launched the Urban Living range in October 2020, months after I left. The system stayed in use for five years and through a change of owner. Other designers executed it. Red Dot cited the fabric in 2021 and the concealed-function archetype in 2023, and by then the business had become Versuni. I have not touched those products. The awarding bodies are the ones describing them.

A design language system is never finished. That it survived the company changing hands is the strongest evidence I have that it was a system rather than a style.

0102016–2020

Philips Air: the Urban Living purifier range

Product designer, Air category

One design language holding across four models, and the detailing that decides whether it holds.

Led the Urban Living air-purifier range across the 2000, 3000 and 4000, and directed the table-top Series 800 through a more junior designer on the team. Delegating the fourth is how a language gets tested: if it only works when I draw it, it is not a system.

The physical product was mine end to end: concept, drawings, CAD, draft angles, down to how many holes make the perforation. On the interface I directed at product level, setting where the display sat, how large it and its icons should be, and the lighting, which I went deep into. The digital designers on the team executed the screens.

CMF sat hand in hand with the product design, with the CMF designer beside me through every review. The move to fabric was my call. Four models built on the same architecture were hard to tell apart by form alone, and a soft material on the intake face separates them by touch and by tone rather than by adding a shape nobody needs.

The fabric outlasted me. A colleague carried the same decision into two products after I had gone, and Red Dot cited it in both.

0132016

Three futures for on-demand mobility: 2020, 2025, 2030

Product designer, BMW Group Designworks

What happens to a car when mobility becomes a service, and whose car it is then.

On-demand mobility was being invented in public, and China was where it was moving fastest: Didi and Uber China were fighting for the market and would merge that August. BMW wanted to know what it would do to the car. The study ran five months from a January kick-off, in the same building as the newly formed ConnectedDrive Lab.

When mobility becomes a service the car stops being one person’s possession and becomes an asset three parties share. The passenger, the driver and the fleet operator, with different interests in the same seat. Almost every study of this kind takes the passenger’s side. Adding the operator is what turns a trend deck into a product brief, because the operator is the one who buys the vehicle.

The fieldwork

Forty participants:

  • sixteen premium passengers
  • sixteen premium drivers
  • eight service-provider experts

across four tier-one cities and one mid-size tourist city, through in-home visits, expert interviews and immersion. That became nine personas, pain-point maps along the whole journey for each party, and three scenarios:

  • Modern Ride Share 2020
  • Car Sharing 2025
  • Chauffeur-less Ride Pooling 2030

The one that made it useful

Two features in the 2020 package sit on the passenger’s list and the driver’s list at the same time: the automatic door, and the alert that says when it is safe to step out. The passenger’s problem is a heavy door and a kerb they cannot read. The driver’s problem is the same door, damaged by daily mistreatment or swung into traffic. One mechanism, two opposed anxieties, resolved together.

Each scenario carried a vehicle concept, and the report closed on a technology roadmap placing every feature against a production programme and a date. Not a trend deck: a product definition with insertion points. It stayed internal and was never announced. What it fed was the technology portfolio of the ConnectedDrive Lab we shared the building with.

About

I am an industrial and product designer. The practice is grounded in physical product development: CMF and prototyping, human-factor research, and the design-language systems that hold a whole range together. Over twenty years it has widened to take in the whole experience of a product, across hardware, software and the way a person handles both. It is an evolution of industrial design rather than a move away from it.

In most product organisations one group decides what to build and another decides how it gets built, and the distance between them is where a product loses its point. I work in that distance. Knowing how a thing is actually made is what earns the right to decide what should be made. I have counted the holes in a perforation and I have sat on the committee that assessed our suppliers, and it is the same job.

Fighting the gravity: doing the right thing at the beginning of the definition.

Instead of going deep into the solution first, go bigger in framing the question. I have had that proved on me twice. A mask drawn for polluted air held when people reached for it in a pandemic. A console definition died with its component in a merger, and the method behind it went straight into the next vehicle. Frame the question well enough and the work outlives the thing it was drawn for.

I live in Reading and work in Bicester. I studied product and industrial design in Taipei, and interactive media arts at New York University, across Shanghai, Berlin and New York. The maker thread has never stopped: bass guitars, code, and small things made for the pleasure of making them.

I mentor inside the teams I work in, and outside them: on ADPList through 2024, international product-design students and early-career designers.

Design is an act of care and responsibility of the human around it.
