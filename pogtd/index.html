Industrial and
product designer.

Twenty years. Six countries. Objects, ranges, systems, ventures and vehicle programmes.

I began by learning how things are made: how a surface meets a mechanism, how a hand finds a control, how a drawing survives contact with tooling. The work has since moved towards questions that exist before a product has settled into its final form.

What should this become?
What must remain true as more people begin to shape it?
What does the person using it actually need to understand, feel or do?

I’m not the solution of the problem, but how I work helps to find that solution.

Reading, United Kingdom.

The work changes scale. The practice does not.

An object can be drawn precisely and still begin with the wrong question.

That becomes more visible as products grow more complex. A product manager may be defining value, an engineer resolving feasibility, a designer giving form to an experience, a supplier proposing what can already be manufactured. Each is making a reasonable decision from a different position. The product forms somewhere among them.

My work begins by making those positions visible.

Sometimes that means sketching. Sometimes it means research, prototypes, technical comparisons, product architecture, or a shared language for judging what belongs. The medium changes according to what the decision requires.

Define the problem before the solution.

Uncertainty can stay open a little longer, as long as it takes enough form for a team to work with it. That is most of what I do: give the unresolved part of a programme a shape people can argue about, early, while changing it is still cheap.

Over twenty years the practice has widened along one line:

Object → direction → design language → range → venture → product and experience definition

The later work depends on the earlier work. Knowing how something is made remains part of deciding what it should be.

The five pieces of work that follow are the same practice at different points on that line.

Selected work 1 · the venture

A product worn on the face must first make room for the person

Philips, Shanghai · 2016–2020 · The Fresh Air Mask, a venture built inside a corporation · product family to 2022.

2016–2020

The insight

A breathing product cannot be designed around an abstract average. Its performance, its comfort and its meaning all meet on the face of one particular person.

Context

In 2016, Philips was exploring how its knowledge of air quality might extend beyond products that clean a room. Outdoor pollution was experienced personally, while the company’s technologies, categories and routes to market were built around indoor environments.

A wearable response raised unfamiliar questions.

Where would the product sit on the face? How would air move through it? What would prolonged contact feel like? Could the parts that get dirty be cleaned or replaced? Would people accept something visibly technological in a social setting? And how would a large organisation develop a product that belonged to none of its established categories?

The work began before there was a settled product brief.

We went looking for the value space around the category, through digital products, wearables and business-to-business ideas, and we came back with a mask. A mask reached the most people, it was personal to own, and it belonged to a company whose business is experience.

Deciding that was the easy half. Pitching it, and keeping it alive inside a large organisation until it shipped, was the venture.

Solution

We treated the proposition as a connected system rather than as a mask with components added to it.

The geometry started from 23,000 Chinese face measurements, recorded on Philips’ own product page. Fit, airflow, filtration, acoustic behaviour, weight, textile construction, electronics and charging were considered together, because a decision in one of them changed the experience of all the others.

Early drawings gave the idea something concrete enough to question. Prototypes then made different questions possible: where pressure accumulated over an hour, how the fan was perceived, what happened when someone tried to speak, which parts felt permanent and which should be replaceable.

The work also had to acquire an organisational form. Incubation followed in 2017. The team was progressive rather than fixed: eight people across the whole journey, each stage with its own venture team behind it. I sat on the venture committee that assessed the design and research capability of suppliers, so supplier knowledge shaped what the product could realistically become instead of arriving after the design was finished.

Fighting the gravity is how I describe that period.

My own role moved with the work: framing the opportunity, defining the product architecture, designing the first product, and helping the group carry an internal proposal into a funded programme.

What emerged was never the direct execution of the original idea. It was the result of letting research, physical trials, technical limits and other people’s expertise alter that idea.

Impact

The first generation went on sale in China in January 2020, three months before I left Philips in March that year.

It had been conceived in response to pollution. During the pandemic, people adopted it in a context none of us had planned for.

That a thing made for everyday air became something people chose to protect their families with is the part of my career I am proudest of.

The family continued without me, and it travelled. A Kids version followed in 2021, and the Fresh Air Mask Lite in 2022, the same architecture with the soft shell removed and the battery moved into the fan module. Colleagues did that work. The product reached international distribution; the UK product page is in the references below.

On the record

I am named as creator/designer in WIPO Hague international design registration families for Breathing Mask and Air Mask designs.

I am a named designer on the Philips Fresh Air Mask project at Dutch Design Week 2020.

I am named as Senior Product Designer in the Philips Fresh Air Mask documentary featured at Dutch Design Week 2020. Philips Experience Design’s own film of the project opens with me presenting it.

The Fresh Air Mask Lite received Red Dot Best of the Best 2022, two years after my departure. That recognition belongs to the team that made it.

Selected work 2 · the design language, and the range

A design language is tested when someone else can use it

Philips Air quality and Homecare · Shanghai and Amsterdam · 2016–2020 · distributed internationally.

2016–2020

The insight

A recognisable family comes from deciding what may change without losing the relationship between the products. Repeating a shape is the easy part.

Context

Philips Air quality and Homecare covered products of different functions, formats, price levels and domestic roles. Each programme had immediate needs of its own, and the collection still had to read as a family.

A styling exercise would have been simpler. The language had to survive different proportions, air paths, interfaces, manufacturing processes and programme teams. It also had to express cleaner air while leaving behind the familiar visual signals of filtration technology.

The question was what these products should have in common.

Solution

We began with the experience of living beside an air product.

These are objects that stay visible and active for many hours at a time. Their scale is closer to furniture than to a handheld appliance, and their technical purpose can make them visually restless: vents, displays, sensors, seams and maintenance access all competing for the same attention.

The design language reduced that restlessness.

The answer was to do less, to provide more.

Function stayed concealed until it was needed. Textile surfaces softened the presence of large air inlets. Interfaces occupied deliberate zones instead of appearing wherever an internal component allowed. Light communicated state while stopping short of turning the product into an instrument panel. Openings, transitions and proportions were governed by a small number of repeatable relationships.

The system worked through three connected layers: the physical product; interaction and light; colour, material and finish. At Philips the interface belongs to product design, so screen behaviour and light behaviour were decided in the same room as the housing, by the people responsible for the housing.

In the Urban Living range I led the design across the 2000, 3000 and 4000, and directed the table-top Series 800 through a more junior designer. Four models, one language.

The fabric call was mine. A soft material on the intake face separated four products of the same architecture by touch and tone, and let them sit in a room as furniture rather than as equipment.

That fourth model mattered most. While I was the one drawing, the language could still have been a personal style. When another designer could interpret it, question it and produce something that still belonged, it had begun to work as a system.

The rules were never a finished manual. Each programme exposed where they were too rigid, too vague, or resting on a decision nobody had written down. The range refined the language while the language gave coherence to the range.

In 2019 the same question was asked at a larger scale. The design leads met in Amsterdam to build one design language for Global Domestic Appliances. I brought a soft-triangle direction; headquarters blended it with a direction coming from homecare. It was carried into the successor company’s portfolio from 2021.

Impact

Philips launched the Urban Living range in October 2020, months after I left. The system stayed in use for five years, through the change of owner to Versuni.

Products released after my departure kept several of the defining relationships: the fabric plane, the restrained technical body, the concealed function, the interaction placed inside the architecture rather than on top of it. Award citations written years later describe those products in the same terms we had used to design the first ones.

I do not claim the later products as my designs. Their continuation is useful for a different reason: it shows which decisions were able to outlive the programme that produced them, and the people who made them.

On the record

I led the Product Design Language System for Philips Air quality and Homecare categories in 2018.

I served as one of the global lead designers for the Global Domestic Appliances Product Design Language System in 2019.

I am named as creator or designer in a Malaysia design registration for graphical user interface designs for air purifier products, held by Versuni Holding B.V.

Continuation, in other people’s words. Versuni was formed from the Philips Domestic Appliances business; the products below were designed by its teams after I left, and I have not touched them.

The fabric detail is still on products dated 2022 and 2024.

Selected work 3 · product and experience definition

A feature is not yet an experience

Forseven and McLaren Automotive · Bicester, United Kingdom · 2024–

2024–

The insight

A product becomes coherent at the moment technical capability is translated into what a person should perceive, understand and do.

Context

On vehicle programmes in the United Kingdom, a product experience is assembled across many specialised functions.

A display may begin as a package and an optical system. A control may begin as a component with defined inputs. Lighting may begin as an available technology. Each can progress a long way technically before the purpose it serves for the driver has been agreed.

This is a consequence of scale rather than a failure of expertise. Each team has to resolve the part it owns.

The unresolved space shows up in questions that belong to the whole car:

What deserves the driver’s attention?
What should be understood at a glance, sensed at the edge of vision, or left quiet?
Which information expresses the character of the car, and which only proves that the system can display it?

Solution

At Forseven I read the platform’s functional definition sheets myself, rather than working from someone else’s summary of them.

The console definition followed from that reading: which functions belong there, how each one should behave, and how a cockpit shared between a driver and a passenger changes both answers. Each behaviour was mapped against the programme’s experience strategy, so that intent and mechanism could be checked against each other, and then validated with human factors.

The component itself did not carry into McLaren after the merger. The method did, straight into the performance HUD for a supercar on the next programme.

At McLaren we are outnumbered roughly ten to one. That ratio is useful discipline: an argument has to be legible to people who did not train in design, or it goes nowhere.

I positioned the performance HUD for a supercar against two references, Gulfstream’s two-layer guidance and the automotive default of replacing the cluster, and took neither. A low roof compresses the viewing port, so the geometry has to answer differently.

What replaced them was a quadrant: confidence, guidance, control and performance, set against the two things a driver actually takes away from the car, which are acuity and achievement. A feature list also has to survive procurement, lead time, manufacturing constraint and cost, so the quadrant doubles as a way of deciding what to defend when something has to go.

Impact

Much of this work is confidential and still in development.

What can be said is the change in the order of decisions: from selecting a capability and finding a use for it, to defining what the driver should get and then judging which capability belongs.

On the record

Forseven, Bicester, 2024–2025. Product Experience Lead: cockpit and body-control definition on an electric-vehicle programme.

McLaren Automotive, Bicester, 2025–. Product Experience Lead: product and experience definition on next-generation programmes.

Selected work 4 · direction, through research

Research is useful when it changes what can be imagined

BMW Group Designworks · Shanghai · 2016

2016

The insight

Research does more than describe people. At its best it changes the range of products a team is able to propose.

Context

In 2016 I spent five months in Shanghai on a study of on-demand mobility in China, running from a January kick-off, in the same building as the newly formed ConnectedDrive Lab.

That year Didi and Uber China were fighting for the same market. They merged that August.

The subject was usually discussed as a relationship between a passenger and a vehicle. In practice the journey involved three parties, and the third one never rides in the car: the fleet operator, who buys the vehicle. Putting the buyer into the frame changes the brief.

Solution

Forty participants:

  • sixteen premium passengers
  • sixteen premium drivers
  • eight service-provider experts

Four tier-one cities and one mid-size tourist city. In-home visits, expert interviews, and immersion in the service itself.

The material became nine personas and three scenarios set at different distances:

  • Modern Ride Share 2020
  • Car Sharing 2025
  • Chauffeur-less Ride Pooling 2030

Each scenario carried a vehicle concept, so the argument stayed physical instead of drifting into slides. The study closed on a technology roadmap that placed features against production programmes and dates.

Two features are the ones I still use to explain the method. An automatic door, and an alert telling a passenger when it is safe to step out, sit on the passenger’s list and the driver’s list at the same time. One moment, read from two sides, resolved once.

Impact

The study stayed internal and was never announced. It fed the technology portfolio of the ConnectedDrive Lab.

Its durable outcome in my own practice was a habit: study the relationships around a product, not only the person touching it.

Work like this often gets filed under foresight. I have always treated it as industrial design done early, which is why every scenario had to arrive as a vehicle you could look at.

Selected work 5 · direction, through colour, material and finish

When the instrument cannot read the market, change the instrument

KISKA · Shanghai · 2023

2023

The insight

A method can look rigorous and still exclude the qualities people actually use to decide.

Context

In 2023, in Shanghai, I was senior design manager for product experience design at KISKA, where I built and led that capability as part of the local management team. It was my last project in China.

Haier wanted a CMF direction for refrigerator door panels aimed at tier-three and tier-four cities, designed from those buyers rather than adapted down from a flagship.

The standard instrument in our field is a palette-and-reference exercise built on mature markets. It classifies very well what it already knows how to see. In these cities it returned a picture with no edges.

The more useful possibility was that the blur belonged to the instrument.

Solution

We changed what we asked.

What people could imagine stopped at the shelf in front of them, so asking them to choose between finished aesthetic propositions could only return what was already on sale. We read aspiration instead: lifestyle and life stage through response to images, then that aspiration translated into the tones, gradients and finishes those buyers read as premium.

Field work ran across Zhejiang, Sichuan and Hebei:

  • twenty-one interviews
  • three market visits
  • nine days in the field, with a core team of three

These cities sit within three to five years of tier-one taste, so the real question was how much newness people would take. Boards were revised live between the three stops and taken out again, which turned the fieldwork into a series of calibrations rather than one reading.

The innovation is going deeper into the local aesthetic, instead of assuming what people want by moving a global aesthetic into local dress.

Impact

Six aesthetic statements, each with a colour and material story and a process that transfers to a production line. Haier reviewed them in October and took the final drafts into their own team to execute.

The most durable outcome was methodological: when research produces a blurred picture, the person being studied is not automatically the source of the blur.

The record

The registrations that name me, the recognition and what I contributed to each, and public references.

Registrations

A registration explains neither the quality of a contribution nor the relationships through which a product was developed. Research, engineering, CMF, interface development, manufacturing and commercialisation all belong to wider teams, and the rights themselves are held by the organisations that commissioned the work.

It records something narrower, and harder to argue with: that particular design work entered an independent public record under my name.

The countries are a poor measure on their own. What they gave me were different conditions to test the practice in: different manufacturing, organisational scale, domestic life, mobility, technical ambition and cultural expectation.

Working across them has produced no universal answer. It has made me slower to assume that one organisation’s vocabulary, one market’s idea of value, or one profession’s definition of the problem is the complete one.

What I am working towards

Industrial design is sometimes invited into a programme after the important assumptions have already become requirements.

The product can still be beautifully resolved. What has narrowed by then is the chance to ask what it should be: whose behaviour it assumes, what kind of attention it asks for, how its parts should belong together.

My current work is about staying with those questions earlier, and making the reasoning around them visible to the people who have to act on it.

That may end as a physical product, an interface, or a family of products. It may also end as a shared definition that keeps three teams from building three different understandings of the same experience.

I remain interested in objects. Interested enough to keep asking what decisions produced them, what systems they sit inside, and what they leave behind with the person who has to live with them.

This is an evolution of industrial design, and it stays inside it.

The work is to recognise when the question has been inherited without examination.

It is to give an uncertain idea enough form to be challenged.

It is to understand what engineering, research, manufacturing, business and design each know, and what none of them can see alone.

It is to leave behind more than a resolved object: a principle another designer can use, a definition a programme can carry forward, or a way of working that stays useful after the original conditions have changed.

About

I live in Reading and work in Bicester. I studied product and industrial design in Taipei, and interactive media arts at New York University, across Shanghai, Berlin and New York. The maker thread has never stopped: I still build musical instruments.

I mentor inside the teams I work in, and outside them: on ADPList through 2024, international product-design students and early-career designers. The intention is never to pass on one method. It is to help someone see what assumption their present method has been hiding from them.
