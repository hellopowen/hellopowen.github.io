Industrial and
product designer.

A body of work beginning in 2006, across six countries. Objects, ranges, systems, ventures and vehicle programmes.

I began by learning how things are made: how a surface meets a mechanism, how a hand finds a control, how a drawing survives contact with tooling. The work has since moved towards questions that exist before a product has settled into its final form.

What should this become?
What must remain true as more people begin to shape it?
What does the person using it actually need to understand, feel or do?

I’m not the solution of the problem, but how I work helps to find that solution.

My work → My record →

The work changes scale. The practice does not.

An object can be drawn precisely and still begin with the wrong question.

That becomes more visible as products grow more complex. A product manager may be defining value, an engineer resolving feasibility, a designer giving form to an experience, a supplier proposing what can already be manufactured. Each is making a reasonable decision from a different position. The product forms somewhere among them.

My work begins by making those positions visible.

Sometimes that means sketching. Sometimes it means research, prototypes, technical comparisons, product architecture, or a shared language for judging what belongs. The medium changes according to what the decision requires.

Define the problem before the solution.

Uncertainty can stay open a little longer, as long as it takes enough form for a team to work with it. That is most of what I do: give the unresolved part of a programme a shape people can argue about, early, while changing it is still cheap.

Since 2006 the practice has unfolded rather than climbed — each piece of work opening the next:

Object → direction → design language → range → venture → whole-product definition

The later work depends on the earlier work. Knowing how something is made remains part of deciding what it should be.

The five pieces of work that follow are the same practice at different points on that line.

Selected work 1 · design venturing

A product worn on the face must first make room for the person

Philips, Shanghai · 2016–2020 · The Fresh Air Mask, a venture built inside a corporation · product family to 2022.

2016–2020

Pollution masks were clunky and heavy, and they clogged with warm moisture from the inside. Those are engineering problems. They were also dull, which is why they stayed in a drawer.

The engineering half held a finding most people get backwards: breathing comfort is governed by exhaling, not inhaling. Getting warm, damp air out is what makes an hour bearable. That one fact decides the architecture later. Performance, comfort and meaning all meet on the face of one particular person, and there is no average face to design for.

We went looking for the value space around the category — digital products, wearables, business-to-business ideas — and came back with a mask. It reached the most people, it was personal to own, and it belonged to a company whose business is experience.

Then it had to be proposed. It went on a stand at the company’s Breakthrough Day in 2017, under the line the pitch was built on: fight pollution in style.

Deciding that was the easy half. Pitching it, and keeping it alive inside a large organisation until it shipped, was the venture.

Incubation followed that year, and eight of us carried it across the whole journey.

The asking did not stop at the pitch. Tested against other masks, people wanted the nose to fit better and the sides not to bulge — and they wanted the technology more visible, not less. We had assumed the technology was the thing to hide; they were wearing it as jewellery. That is why the fan is exposed.

We treated it as a connected system rather than a mask with parts added to it. Fit, airflow, filtration, acoustics, weight, textile, electronics and charging were decided together, because changing one of them changed the experience of all the others. Splitting the fan and the battery into separate chambers balanced the weight across the face, and the same split improved exhaling, because the space it freed was the space the breath needed. Three connections hold it together: a magnetic battery fixing, a sliding filter hook, a locking ring for the fan.

The geometry was built on five anchor points taken from facial structure and turned into a parametric model, so that one shell could hold the variation across the 23,000 face measurements recorded on Philips’ own product page, rather than average it away.

The soft shell took four attempts, and three of them failed usefully. Cut-and-sewn needed too many seams to make a curved cup, and the labour cost came with them. A bonded preform left the edges too rigid to hold a smooth volume, and the material smelled. The route that held was thermoplastic yarn: we studied the ideal amount of hot melt yarn necessary to create a supportive shell with a soft feel, and the route that held was thermoplastic yarn, and that structure is what the utility model covers. The later Lite version is an adjacent proposition rather than a descendant: it borrows an existing filter as its structure, carries the combined fan and battery on one side, and does not use the patented shell. That shell is what the utility patent covers — the structure that holds the mask together, holding the filter and holding the fan.

Founding engagement meant the whole scope: the product architecture, the resources we had to go and get, and how the design intent survived quality and manufacturing. When we needed facial-scan data turned into a parametric model, we went to SRC inside Philips for the tool and built our own mesh on top of it to fit Asian faces.

The first generation went on sale in China in January 2020, three months before I left Philips. It had been conceived in response to pollution. During the pandemic, people adopted it in a context none of us had planned for. That a thing made for everyday air became something people chose to protect their families with is the part of my career I am proudest of.

The number that stands for all of it is 59 grams — basically two pairs of acetate optical glasses. That is what an hour of wearing it feels like.

Selected work 2 · scaling a promise

Turning three series into one range, and a language the business could grow with

Philips, Shanghai and Amsterdam · 2018–2019 · The Air quality and Homecare product design language system, and the global Domestic Appliances language.

2018–2019

A brand can change what it promises in a sentence. Making that promise credible takes a range of products, and something strong enough to hold them together.

Philips Air had three series — 2000, 3000, 4000 — grown up separately and looking it: different sizes, different shapes, different architectures, different propositions. They were the category’s core, and most of that core sold online in China. One supplier could carry all three, and that made a hypothesis worth testing: replace three unrelated designs with one tiered range — big, medium, small — sharing an architecture and scaling to the size of the room, so a different clean air delivery rate is a size in the same family rather than a different product.

A hypothesis is not a plan. Engineering had to show that an architecture rescales to spec and not only in principle. The channels had to accept it. And the language had to be found rather than issued: nobody hands you a unified design language with a business case. It is what you arrive at if the same architecture is going to carry three sizes. Unification was not the goal, only the hypothesis. You can hold the large and the medium in one language and let the small stand apart, if the business works, and deciding which of those the business can carry is the job. A design language system sits between design management and design strategy: a shared set of rules, enough to make products belong together, loose enough to let each one do its own work.

I led the Product Design Language System for Philips Air quality and Homecare categories in 2018.

The promise was moving at the same time. Healthy air, always was clinical, hygienic, professional. It was becoming better air, better quality of life — a wider claim, because the argument for clean air at home is not only the pollution outside; it is the allergens and the dust in the room. I was not in the meeting where that was decided. My job was to turn a sentence I did not write into a section, a material and a perforation. Three pillars came out of the workshops with engineering, research, business and the science group, one of them approachable professional, and one lifestyle approach we kept returning to: simple and inviting, yet with a clear innovative edge. We always say reassurance in Philips: you are reassured by the solution, so you don’t have to worry. A machine in a living room says something about its owner. We changed what it said.

The first instinct was to evolve what had already worked. One early concept drew hard on the 2-in-1 purifier and humidifier — a Red Dot Best of the Best, and the strongest thing the category had done — and it was engineering as much as design asking whether we could carry that shape across. It would have made the unification claim easier. But the 2-in-1 was a different architecture answering a different promise: a multifunctional object doing two jobs, where this range does one, against pollution and allergens, as a tower read from across a room rather than off a worktop. A successful shape does not transfer just because it was successful. Borrowing it without the layers underneath would have left us with a resemblance and nothing to build the rest on. Working that out is where the Air language actually started.

The category itself resisted. Every tower purifier looks the same from across a room: a tube with a cap, a muted upper body, and a bottom carrying all the visual weight, because that is where the intake perforation is. Square, soft square or round in section, at three metres they are one product. And our own history was part of it — the previous language always went square with rounded corners, and we kept returning to it, because a squircle section was Philips and Philips was professional. We had gated ourselves. It was not getting us to the new promise, so we abandoned it before we had anything to put in its place.

The range was also not three products. It was six. Most of the core sold through China’s two large online platforms, and each wanted a range of its own — the same three sizes, visibly different products. Two sub-ranges inside one tiered range, one language holding all six while still keeping them apart.

One product you can develop with three concepts and three months to master an idea. A range with two sub-ranges and tiered sizes needs something more open — more ideas on the table before anything settles, because what the room is choosing is not a product. It is a rule six products will have to live under. So twenty concepts went up, with furniture names, IKEA names, so people argued about types of idea rather than drawings. The whole company voted, and what came back was a signal to read against the architecture, the promise and both channels rather than a verdict.

The answer came off a factory floor. Other brands were beginning to use fabric on purifiers, as a pre-filter sleeve at the bottom — a trouser pulled over the intake, which goes slack over time and looks it. Going to fabric was my call: nothing else was going to separate us at three metres, and the material was right where only the position was wrong. So we kept it and inverted it. The texture moved to the upper body, at hand height on a tower — the human touchpoint, not a functional part. The perforation stayed at the bottom and was laid out to fade, dissolving upward into the fabric and downward into the base, at diameters between 2.8 and 3.8 millimetres. A single diameter made the field read as a patch of grey. Corners were softened symmetrically. The section stayed round — always round, because a round section is what the architecture runs most efficiently on. None of that is only a design decision: a fabric body has to be made, fitted, cleaned and costed, so the moment the language asked for it, engineering and the supplier were solving it too.

The same material then answered the channel problem: wrapped horizontally for one platform’s range, run vertically up the front for the other’s, which pushes the shape into a trapezoid. Two sub-ranges visibly apart, unmistakably one family. A jury later described a fabric panel along the front and read it as accessibility and friendliness. That was the intent, arriving in someone else’s words.

The 2000, 3000 and 4000 were on my board, twice over. The 800 was not — a junior designer drew the desktop version, smaller and tapered towards the top, for a shelf or a bedside. My part there was the tolerances: how far the curve could go before it stopped being the language.

A language that holds from a floor-standing tower down to a desktop unit drawn by someone else — applied, not copied — is a language. Anything less is a theme.

I served as one of the global lead designers for the Global Domestic Appliances Product Design Language System in 2019.

The following year the same question was asked across the whole portfolio. Philips Experience Design convened five lead designers in Amsterdam under three design directors, covering garment and floor care, air and kitchen, and coffee; three of us travelled from the Shanghai team. We read every category through the same five lenses — form, detail, interface and light, colour and material, branding — then made a second pass that kept only what survived all five. Taken separately, the categories wanted different things: floor care wanted strong shoulders and a softer under-belly; coffee wanted transparency to halo the ingredients. And kitchen wanted the squircle we had just abandoned, for its own good reasons. What they shared was smaller and more useful — fabric or transparency, a light signature, a grip that tells your hand where to go, and technology expressed honestly rather than hidden.

Above the lenses sat four horizons: where a product sits in a home, premium and value, one Domestic Appliances, one Philips. And the room was honest about the cost. The challenges went up beside the benefits — it cannot be done overnight; the business has to stop thinking in categories; different form languages end up in the same room; and someone has to make sure the team actually applies the guidelines.

Back in Shanghai we produced two proposals with the wider design group, each carrying every category. I built one of the two we took to Amsterdam, where the directions from both studios were synthesised into the single system, carried through the business, and resolved into final renders and models. A design language is only a system if it works without you.

The system document issued in 2020. In 2021 I was brought back as an independent consultant through the separation of the Domestic Appliances business — now Versuni — and the concept collection went across with it, into a new owner’s factories, where products could be built to a language that already carried the brand’s credibility. A cooker, a sandwich maker: they did not have to earn the promise again. That is what a design language is for, and it is why a theme is not the same thing.

The image has not changed since 2020 — Versuni still presents Philips with that collection, a purifier on a raised base, in a speckled fabric body, with the badge on the fabric. It does not need to. An image that can be explained, argued and applied is a guiding star; the execution underneath it keeps moving, closer each year to what the business needs and what the customer expects. Static at the top and dynamic underneath is what a working system looks like.

The promise began as a sentence: better air, better quality of life. The work was to make it visible in a silhouette, tangible in a fabric panel, and durable enough to guide products I would never design myself.

Selected work 3 · tangible intelligence

Tangible intelligence, and high performance acuity — expanding capability without adding more

Forseven → McLaren Automotive · Bicester · 2024– · Product definition.

2024–

The physical world inside a car is fixed, and in a performance car it is shrinking, while digital capability keeps growing. Both halves of this work sit on that tension. The answer is not more controls and not more screen: it is a curated physical control you can actually sense, and a display disciplined by what the car is.

I used to put my hands on the shift gear. The console carries that inheritance: it is the one control surface that belongs to everyone in the car, not only to the person driving. And the centre rotary becomes a tapping control — navigating a digital interface the way software has always been navigated, moving from top left to bottom right.

A software-defined system arrives with more digital functions than a panel can hold, and most of them need no dedicated control at all. We decided to keep a few of them physical anyway, and that decision is where the difficulty starts. The moment you keep a control, you have to work out how it lives with two displays: what the layers of experience are, what happens with no press, what happens on press, what happens on press and hold, and which function belongs in which layer.

I staged the experience layers that make sense of the product interaction and the controls, and worked out what type of control would bridge advanced software and hardware — so the cabin stays familiar and is elevated by what sits underneath it. The strongest input was never the functionality. It was the customer, the client and the context: what they would want to have in the car.

The control, the display between the seats and the display in the driver’s sightline above it are aligned on one line, and that alignment is the opportunity. The first layer asks for no press at all. Press, and you are controlling what sits on the display between the seats. Press and hold, and voice opens across the display in the driver’s line.

One control. Two displays. Three layers of experience.

This is not adding more in order to distract the driver. It is one modality, proposed as a product concept, to show what two displays and a control can do together: delight, captivate, and grow with the person using them. Those three are not marketing verbs here; they are the guiding experience principles we used to bridge the physical and the digital — a familiar console anchoring a software-defined system that could otherwise live entirely inside a display.

We staged media, audio and voice against press and hold, so that a hardware assumption stopped being an assumption and became something you could justify. I developed the hypotheses and interaction concepts with UX and human factors, showed them through three scenarios, and took them to senior stakeholders as low-fidelity demonstrations — because a panel of this kind is an investment decision before it is a design one. After the senior stakeholders agreed, the work went into human-factors testing — so a hardware assumption became something you could justify. The concepts passed senior review and entered human-factors testing; nothing from this programme is public, and I make no claim about what ships.

The display work is the same tension under a harder constraint, and there the tagline is high performance acuity. A performance car sits low, and its viewport is compressed. Most of the industry treats the driver’s sightline as room for a full infotainment surface; in a car shaped like this, that covers up the view the driver came for. The constraint is not something to work around. It is the argument.

So the questions become what capability actually belongs there, what the value proposition is, and how the few things that survive should be laid out.

I wrote the product concept brief for the performance HUD for a supercar: what it should give the driver, and by what measure. Three goals filtered every candidate — one look is enough; the driver acts rather than interprets; the car becomes legible, not louder. The conclusion was a product defined by subtraction, not screen size.

To make the judgment measurable, I scored every candidate feature against the same quadrant — performance, control, guidance, confidence — and beside it ran an acuity computation: how much time the driver has to react, multiplied by how badly it matters if they mistime it, with a written rationale for every row. More features left the definition than entered it. That was not simply subtraction; it was routing — failing on one axis sent a candidate to the surface that suited it instead.

Working with a physical constraint while the capability behind it keeps growing is the same job in both halves. Done well it does not produce a longer feature list. It produces fewer moments that matter more.

And they feel like the brand they belong to — with the argument behind them written down, from the whole panel to a single rotary, so that a later reader can disagree with a row and say why.

Selected work 4 · design foresight

Local-leading research on the future of the shared ride

BMW Group Designworks · Shanghai · 2015–2016 · The On-Demand Mobility study.

2015–2016

A car sold into a shared ride is not bought the way a car is bought. The decision is made by the whole system around the vehicle — the service, the infrastructure behind it, the operator running the fleet, the app that dispatches it, and the comfort of the particular car that turns up.

Three parties hold an interest in the same seat: the passenger, the driver, and the operator who owns the fleet and pays for the wear. The vehicle design was a carrier to showcase all of these.

Foresight is about setting a goal for the future, and about setting a scope for ourselves. The way you do that is a scenario: who is using it, what a day is like for them, and what that day could be instead.

The brief arrived already asking to be answered three times over — short-term, mid-term, long-term. What we chose was what those three horizons would contain. China was where they could be asked at all: nowhere else was the behaviour further ahead.

The ODM work is really about synthesis. The research established the touchpoints, the pain points, and who takes part in the journey — the operator, the driver, the passenger. The value is in how you pick up the work your team has built and lay a fabric around it: reconnecting those points in a meaningful way, against three horizons and the vehicle implications of each, until separate dots become one connected picture.

Three days came out of it.

The first is a business trip, which still has a driver. The traveller is properly received: the car knows they are coming; the cabin is theirs for the half-hour; a conversation stays inside their seat; stepping out is safer with a scooter coming up the inside. The driver’s half of the same journey is a design problem of its own — preparing to serve the customer a little better, using the limited features the car actually has.

The second is an individual trip in a car nobody owns, small enough to leave on a street. Its whole benefit is that an anonymous vehicle stops being anonymous: it recognises you at the kerb, carries your settings, and lets you switch the camera off with your hand rather than a menu.

The third is a family collected from several places at once, in something closer to an MPV, with no driver in the frame. One cloud holds several personal settings and deploys them into the same car, zone by zone. The profile travels; the car does not.

Across the three futures, one small thing keeps changing hands: the greeting. In the near scenario a human driver welcomes you with a message on the car window. In the middle one the window shows your own avatar. In the far one a virtual chauffeur appears and greets you. The driver is designed out of the journey — and the greeting is still there.

None of this was solving a problem. It was illustrating futures — making three scenarios concrete enough to argue with: forty-one proposals, each carrying the human difficulty it answers, quoted back from the research, and all of them ranked on one page by how often they would be used and how much they matter to a shared ride. Priority one was the near, deliverable scenario. A foresight study that ranks its nearest work highest is one that expected to be used.

It was an internal guiding product, and the presentation was well received. At group level, the questions being explored were about brands and platforms. This study was about seats and conditions: what a driver-led service requires, what changes when the passenger becomes the driver, and what happens when there is no driver at all. The two pieces of work approached some of the same touchpoints at different scales, from opposite ends.

What stayed with me from it is a habit: study the relationships around a product, not only the person touching it.

Selected work 5 · designing for life stages

Shaping a local narrative instead of imposing ours

KISKA, Shanghai · 2023 · Senior Design Manager, Product Experience Design · colour, material and finish for a domestic-appliance client, Haier.

2023

The palette we had learned in mature markets did not work here. Not worked less well — it did not read at all. The innovation is going deeper into the local aesthetic, instead of assuming what people want by moving a global aesthetic into local dress.

And the thing we were reading was not a lifestyle. Lifestyle is a bit metropolitan, a bit urban. What these buyers cared about was which stage they were at, and which image they echoed with — where their life was going, and what it would look like when it arrived. That is not a softer version of the same question. It became the instrument: what the fieldwork measured, city by city, was 生活狀態 — the life state someone is in, and the one they are moving towards.

The buying decision told us where to aim. It begins three to five metres from the shelf and closes with a hand on the door: colour first, at a distance; then gloss or matte; then pattern; then texture; and only at arm’s length, price, function, size and brand. A colour decision made at five metres is not a colour decision. It is a decision about whether this object belongs in the life you are heading towards.

The plan was nine interviews, online. We ran twenty-one, in person, across three provinces. A life state is not something you can ask about down a video call.

In the room, we co-created with the interviewees: they identified themselves in the image we provided — a montage where we blended detail and portrait and colours and aspiration into one picture — and then talked about which elements in it aligned with their life stage at this point. So it was an interview, and it was also back and forward. They were not choosing between finished designs; they were taking apart a picture of a life and telling us which pieces of it were theirs.

We brought them to us: a venue in each city rather than the doorstep. We interviewed dealers separately, because they see what actually leaves the shop. We walked the shops in every city and recorded what was on display as a finding in its own right. Three cities, three round trips out of Shanghai — 義烏 in Zhejiang, 江油 in Sichuan, 保定 in Hebei — nine days across July and some six thousand kilometres, with a core team of three.

We translated the mood boards from very colourful to less saturated, so that people could feel connected to their own life image. We took the imagery from the desk research, added imagery of the life each direction described, then mined the combined picture for its colour, its pattern, its texture. The palette came out of a picture of a life. We were not imposing an aesthetic around their life to say they have to be someone else, or dragging a global language into a lower-tier city in China to say this is a language you need to accept.

We rebuilt the boards between the cities, not after the fieldwork. The protocol grew with each city: four interviews, then four with a survey behind them, then thirteen. One of the six directions was replaced mid-fieldwork.

The one built for older households never appeared in a single city’s results. A young-family direction that existed in no desk research at all first showed up in the second city’s answers. The instrument changed while we were using it.

What came back was a set of aesthetic statements — 審美主張 — each pairing a validated life state with a sentence in the buyer’s own voice, a colour story, a finish call, a pattern call, and production routes established separately with materials experts so that the aesthetic could actually be made. Two matrices then plotted every statement against market position and production cost, and against age, household income and breadth of appeal — giving the client a way to weigh aesthetic relevance, commercial reach and feasibility as a portfolio rather than as six preferences.

The client reviewed the work at concept stage in October and returned written feedback on each concept. We revised the set item by item, extended it, and re-specified it against a cost target, and the final scheme design drafts went across to the Haier team to execute.

It was my last project in China. The final work stayed with Haier; the method stayed with me — we do a little bit, we test it, we show it and ask how this makes your life better, and then we do a little bit more.

The countries are a poor measure on their own. What they gave me were different conditions to test the practice in: different manufacturing, organisational scale, domestic life, mobility, technical ambition and cultural expectation.

Working across them has produced no universal answer. It has made me slower to assume that one organisation’s vocabulary, one market’s idea of value, or one profession’s definition of the problem is the complete one.

What I am working towards

Industrial design is sometimes invited into a programme after the important assumptions have already become requirements.

The product can still be beautifully resolved. What has narrowed by then is the chance to ask what it should be: whose behaviour it assumes, what kind of attention it asks for, how its parts should belong together.

My current work is about staying with those questions earlier, and making the reasoning around them visible to the people who have to act on it.

That may end as a physical product, an interface, or a family of products. It may also end as a shared definition that keeps three teams from building three different understandings of the same experience.

I remain interested in objects. Interested enough to keep asking what decisions produced them, what systems they sit inside, and what they leave behind with the person who has to live with them.

This is an evolution of industrial design, and it stays inside it.

The work is to recognise when the question has been inherited without examination.

It is to give an uncertain idea enough form to be challenged.

It is to understand what engineering, research, manufacturing, business and design each know, and what none of them can see alone.

It is to leave behind more than a resolved object: a principle another designer can use, a definition a programme can carry forward, or a way of working that stays useful after the original conditions have changed.

About

I live in Reading and work in Bicester. I studied product and industrial design in Taipei, and interactive media arts at New York University, across Shanghai, Berlin and New York. The maker thread has never stopped: I still build musical instruments.

I mentor inside the teams I work in, and outside them: on ADPList through 2024, international product-design students and early-career designers. The intention is never to pass on one method. It is to help someone see what assumption their present method has been hiding from them.
