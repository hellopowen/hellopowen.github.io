01 / Philips Experience Design, Shanghai / role 2016—2020 · venture 2017—2020

Designing protection people would choose to wear.

A mask has to protect, but protection only works when people are willing to wear it. Anatomy, breathing, fit, weight, material, visible technology and personal style had to become one wearable product rather than a hard technical shell held against the face.

Breathing comfort depends on exhaling, so warm, damp air needed a route out. Consumer work added another requirement: the technology had to be visibly useful. The fan did not need to disappear; its presence made the function legible and became part of how the mask was socially read.

The fan and battery were split into separate chambers to balance weight and release space for exhalation. Five facial anchor points identified where the product could take load without making the face uncomfortable. Informed by facial-scan data, those points became a parametric geometry that could work with variation rather than an average face.

Fit, airflow, filtration, acoustics, textile, electronics, charging and social meaning had to resolve together.

Softness had to be engineered.

The soft shell took four attempts. Cut-and-sewn construction needed too many seams; a bonded preform held the wrong rigidity and odour. The thermoplastic-yarn route let us tune softness and structural support through the yarn composition, mould temperature and the behaviour of male and female tooling. The shell had to remain comfortable against the face while carrying the internal components.

Material was both utility and expression. The same development joined knitting, edge construction, breathability, supplier samples and manufacturing with the way the product looked and felt when worn.

I was the product designer in the eight-person founding venture team, working from the 2017 pitch through the January 2020 launch. The scope crossed idea pitch, commercialisation, product development, supply-chain integration and realisation.

02 / Philips Air + Homecare / Shanghai + Amsterdam / 2018—2019

A design language for air care at home.

Philips Air had met the market through “Healthy air, always”: a clinical and professional outlook. By 2018 the promise had moved to “Better air, better quality of life.” Three supplier architectures were also being brought into one scalable base. The next range had to carry both changes.

A common architecture made the range possible and made sameness more likely. The products still had to separate into two channel-specific sub-ranges, across three sizes, without losing the family.

At a distance, a purifier communicates through silhouette, proportion and the light ring that makes air quality visible. Closer, the control comes into reach. At touch distance, material, perforation and grille detail take over. We designed the range across those distances rather than treating form, interface and CMF as separate layers.

Fabric was positioned on the upper body as a human touchpoint, where the product is seen and a hand naturally reaches. The functional intake remained below. A perforation field between 2.8 and 3.8 millimetres dissolved into the fabric above and the base below, while the top surface brought outlet, control and feedback together.

The decision gave products that could converge at a distance a different domestic presence. It also gave two sub-ranges room to move: one wrapping the material horizontally, the other carrying it vertically through a more tapered body.

03 / Philips Experience Design / Amsterdam + Shanghai / 2019

One design language across unlike categories.

Domestic appliances could not share one silhouette. Air, kitchen appliances, garment care and floor care each had their own architecture, use context and reason to look the way they did.

External context / Versuni Philips home-appliance brand context Open source ↗

“From coffee machines to air purifiers, for over 130 years, Philips have delivered innovative products that create healthier and happier lives.”

Versuni · Philips brand context ↗

Philips Design has repeatedly made a changing view of life and technology tangible—from Vision of the Future in 1996 to Next Simplicity in 2005/06. The philosophy evolved rather than preserving one look. In the 2019 language work, the scale was the product family: begin with the whole form, then work inward through detail, material, interaction, light and graphics.

The common framework read each category through those five layers. What could become a shared perceptual or experiential principle was carried across; what made a category credible remained specific.

The framework stayed constant while each answer changed. Air could move from professional to approachable through its raised base, visible air semantics, circular light and fabric. Coffee could make process visible through transparency, amber light and ingredient focus.

The work joined category direction, interaction, CMF and brand around a common set of principles and a tangible guideline. My contribution covered Air in the Amsterdam formulation and part of the collection work developed in Shanghai; the final synthesis was shared work.

The system was issued across Domestic Appliances. After I left Philips, a separate 2021 engagement brought me back to apply the language to categories Philips had not produced before. The same five lenses could guide new work without forcing it into an existing form.

04 / KISKA, Shanghai / 2023

From local life stages to refrigerator CMF.

Six global trend directions began as hypotheses, not answers. In Jiangyou, Yiwu and Baoding, the research asked how people described home, aspiration and the next stage of their lives—and what made a refrigerator belong there.

The interview material brought people, interiors, activities, colour, material and aspiration into one montage. Participants could take it apart: what belonged to their lives now, what did not, and what they were moving towards.

After the first conversations, we reduced the saturation and rebuilt the imagery around what people had taught us: work and family setting, where they had come from, whether they hoped to buy a home, and what came next. Returning to Shanghai between cities let the next conversation go further instead of repeating a board that had already reached its limit.

The six directions became six aesthetic statements grounded in customer language. Expert material and process input then helped translate them into colour, finish, pattern, texture and transferable production possibilities.

The final proposals applied all six directions to refrigerators and compared their role across a portfolio. They were delivered as CMF and graphic directions; no adoption or market result is claimed.

05 / BMW Group Designworks, Shanghai / 2015—2016

Developing product and service futures for on-demand mobility.

App-based ride-hailing was expanding in the mid-2010s, but the journey involved more than booking a car. It began before the passenger reached the vehicle and continued through the app, driver and fleet operator. The project asked what that relationship could become in 2020, 2025 and 2030.

BMW Group context · 2012—2018

My part began when the field research came back to the studio. We reorganised the insights around passenger, driver and operator, then built a journey for each horizon.

Pain points were placed along ordering, finding, entering, travelling and leaving the vehicle; product and service proposals responded where they were needed. Together they formed a vehicle experience package for each horizon: the service, digital identity and physical vehicle considered as one journey.

Access, identity, privacy, personal settings, climate, comfort, cleaning and maintenance only became useful when attached to a point in the journey. Recognition shows how that relationship changed across the three horizons.

In 2020, passenger and driver had to identify one another and a message on the car supported the welcome. In 2025, a wearable guided the customer to a booked car carrying their chosen avatar. By 2030, cloud identity let a virtual chauffeur recognise and greet a family, while individual seats supported personal audio, climate and content. Trust depended on recognition in both directions.

After the journeys and proposals, one matrix for each horizon compared usage frequency with relevance to on-demand mobility. It was a prioritisation summary, not the whole method. Vehicle overviews then returned the product and service priorities to a physical package.

06 / Current practice / United Kingdom / 2024—

Defining multimodal interaction in software-defined programmes.

In a software-defined programme, capability keeps growing after the cabin package is fixed. For the centre-console switch panel, the opportunity was to make that capability useful to both driver and passenger through one control and two displays. Product definition connected the control, digital functions and three experience layers before the interaction settled into hardware.

For a performance HUD, the constraint was a reduced supercar viewport. We decided not to treat it as an extension of infotainment or repeat the instrument cluster's numerical language. A minimal graphical approach does more with less while following the same interaction principles.

About
